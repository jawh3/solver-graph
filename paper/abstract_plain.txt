A self-extending memory lets an LLM agent reuse what it has learned, but consolidating its own experience can degrade performance when the agent encodes causally-incorrect lessons - false-lesson propagation. We study this in a verifiable-reward domain, Capture-The-Flag, and ask: can the verifiable flag alone - no gold trajectory, no human label, no LLM judge - tell a genuinely useful lesson from a causally-false (poison) one the agent executes and recovers from? We map the boundary on a controlled testbed whose lesson causality is known by construction. The agent's stochastic retrieval is a free randomized experiment - each lesson's inclusion is a draw with a logged propensity - so inverse-propensity weighting over its own logs recovers, at no extra inference, the large value of helpful lessons that outcome credit conflates with poison. Our central result is negative: the per-item harm of a benign-false lesson is not identifiable from outcomes. Neither the cheap estimate nor an assumption-light leave-one-out gold standard can rank it (gold-standard mean +0.045, 95% CI [-0.006,+0.098]), and the mildly-negative observational sign is confounded, not the causal estimand. We give a scoped argument why: under a measured base-rate floor, executing a benign-wrong recipe is, for the binary outcome, nearly equivalent to executing none. A positive control delimits what can be caught: actively-destructive lessons are detected, a confidently-wrong answer is untested (the agent almost never ran it), and a verifier recovers recipe validity by construction. "Non-identifiable" means not per-item-rankable, not "harmless": a small sub-floor harm persists when poison runs, and the null is scoped to one crypto-decode microbenchmark (a second, SQL, one breaks it; a third degenerates). The contribution is this boundary, stated honestly, plus a released, contamination-free, ground-truth-labeled instrument.
